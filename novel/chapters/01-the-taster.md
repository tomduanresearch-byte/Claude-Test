# Chapter 1: The Taster

Every pill in the Azure Furnace Sect was tasted before it was sold, and for nine years the tongue that tasted them had belonged to Shen Mo. He had a ledger to prove it: four hundred and twelve pages of cheap bamboo paper, stitched together with thread pulled from his own sleeves. It recorded every pill that had gone down his throat and everything it had done to him on the way.

*Entry 3,961,* he wrote that morning, while the other tasters were still coughing themselves awake on the benches. *Spleen-Warming Pill. Herb Hall batch, low Mortal grade. Taste: honey, dried tangerine peel, too much licorice (cheap licorice; they are cutting it again). Effect: mild warmth in the stomach. Dregs: slight. Grey, fine, settles in the lower left meridians.*

He paused with the brush over the page. Then he added, because the ledger was the only place he allowed himself to be honest: *Tastes like the ones from when I was eight. Someone in the Herb Hall still uses the old recipe. I like them.*

He tucked the book inside his robe, against his ribs, where it always lived.

The Tasting Hall sat at the very bottom of Pill Peak, where the runoff from a hundred furnaces collected in stone gutters and steamed in the cold. It was a long, low room of damp grey stone with twelve benches, twelve basins, and a brass bell that nobody had ever polished. The alchemists said it had been built low for drainage. The tasters said it had been built low because poison runs downhill, and so does everything else.

"Brother Shen." Xu Ping was sweeping, as he did every morning, as if sweeping would get him noticed and promoted. He was twelve and had been a taster for three months, which meant he still believed that. "Old Tong's coughing grey again."

"I heard." Shen Mo had heard it all night: the wet, heavy rattle of a man whose meridians were filling up like a drainpipe full of silt. Every pill left something behind. The alchemists called it *dan zha*, dregs: the impurities of the herbs and the scars of the fire, settling in the body's channels like river mud. Inner disciples paid fifty contribution points to have theirs flushed out. Tasters earned two a month.

So tasters silted, until one morning a taster simply didn't wake up, and Steward Kang wrote *reassigned* on the roster and sent for a new child.

Most tasters lasted two years. Shen Mo had lasted nine. He had been stuck at the first layer of Qi Condensation since the age of ten, his meridians so thick with other people's leftovers that qi moved through them like cold honey. In place of a future he had his tongue, his ledger, and a very clear idea of which pills would kill a man and which would only make him wish they had.

"Give Old Tong the warm bench by the gutter," he said. "And the Spleen-Warming pills, if any come down today. He can have my share."

Xu Ping grinned. "You always say that like it's an order."

"It is an order."

"You're not a steward."

"No," Shen Mo agreed. "I'm something much more dangerous. I'm the one who knows where the steward hides his wine."

---

The door opened at the second bell, and the hall went quiet in a way Shen Mo had not heard in years.

Steward Kang came first, as always, carrying the lacquered tray: a thick-necked man in his forties, Foundation Establishment, with a face like a clenched fist. Behind him came a young woman in the blue-and-white robes of a Pill Hall inner disciple, with a slate and a brush, her sleeves tied back like someone who actually worked a furnace. Everyone knew her by reputation: Lin Qiao, Elder Gu's most gifted apprentice, Foundation Establishment at seventeen, said to be rude to elders and right about it.

Behind her, stooping slightly under the low lintel, came Elder Gu Hanshan himself.

The head of the Pill Hall did not come to the Tasting Hall. Results went up the mountain on slips of paper, pills came down on trays, and that was the arrangement. Gu was a tall, thin man in plain grey, three hundred years old and looking fifty, with eyes that rested on things the way a scale rests on its weights.

Those eyes passed over the twelve tasters as if counting them.

Kang cleared his throat and read from the slate Lin Qiao handed him. "Nine-Turn Marrow Pill. Trial batch, Earth grade. For Senior Brother Pei's Foundation Establishment, ten days from now. You've been honored, all of you." He said *honored* the way other men said *fined*. "Taster: Xu Ping."

The broom stopped.

Shen Mo did not look at the boy. He looked at the tray. On black lacquer, alone in a nest of white silk, sat a pill the colour of old ivory, the size of a quail's egg, faintly ringed like the cross-section of a tree. Nine rings.

An Earth-grade pill. A Foundation Establishment pill, built to wash the marrow of a disciple at the ninth layer of Qi Condensation, the very peak of the realm. Xu Ping was at the second layer. The first turn would open his marrow. The third would split his meridians like bamboo in a fire.

Everyone in the hall knew it. No one in the hall was going to say it.

"Steward," Shen Mo said, standing, "may this one ask why?"

Kang's eyes found him with real pleasure. "The boy's meridians are clean. Elder Gu wants a clean reading."

"A clean reading from a dead boy isn't worth very much." Shen Mo kept his voice mild and his head bowed, the way you handle a dog you don't know. "His channels won't hold the first turn of a Foundation pill. You'll get a reading of burst meridians. You'll have to repeat the test tomorrow, and you'll have one fewer taster to repeat it with."

"And you would do better?"

"My meridians are silted, Steward. Which is why I'll live long enough to tell you what it does."

Kang's hand rose, and Shen Mo braced for the blow. Better the left ear; it was already half deaf.

"Let him."

Elder Gu had not raised his voice. He did not need to. Kang's hand stopped in the air as though it had struck glass.

"Elder," Lin Qiao said quickly, "his dregs will contaminate the reading. We won't be able to tell what the pill does from what his silting does."

"Then you will learn to read through contamination." Gu's gaze settled on Shen Mo, weighing. "Most disciples who buy this pill will be silted, Disciple Lin. A pill that only works on clean meridians is a pill for almost no one." He inclined his head a fraction of an inch toward the bench. "The silted ones last longer. Longer is useful. Proceed."

Behind Shen Mo, very quietly, Xu Ping let out a breath. Shen Mo did not turn around. He tapped the ledger through his robe twice, which meant *later*, and walked to the testing bench.

---

The protocol never changed. Palm flat on the jade pulse-plate, which woke under his hand and glowed a thin, sluggish green: the colour of his qi, the colour of a pond with too much weed in it. Pill on the tongue. Three breaths. Name what you taste. Swallow. Call out the effects as they come.

Lin Qiao stood across the bench with her brush poised. There was an old burn scar across the back of her hand. She was watching the pill, not him, and Shen Mo found he didn't mind. It was the first time in nine years anyone from the Pill Hall had looked at a pill he was tasting as though it mattered.

He put it on his tongue.

First breath. The outer ring was already softening, as Earth-grade pills did, keyed to the warmth of a living mouth.

"Blood-marrow ginseng," he said, around it. "Forty years, not thirty. Tiger-bone vine, fresh, not dried. White jade lotus seed." He frowned. "Lotus seed that's been toasted first. That's unusual."

Lin Qiao's brush stopped for half a heartbeat. Then it moved again, faster.

Second breath. The flavor opened, rich and heavy as marrow broth simmered overnight.

"Cold-spring water from the north well, not the east. The east well tastes of copper. Nine-leaf dragon's-tongue grass. That's the green." He let it sit. The formula was clean and elegant, balanced like a good knot. "That's everything," he started to say.

Third breath.

Under the broth, under the iron and the green, faint as a thread in a bolt of silk, there was something else. It was bitter and dry, like the wick of a candle a moment after it's been pinched out.

Shen Mo knew that taste. He had tasted it once before, when he was eleven, in a batch of Blood-Clotting Pills that had killed three tasters on the benches beside him in a single afternoon. Afterward the Law Hall had found the cause, and a furnace boy of fourteen had been beheaded for it.

The ledger called it, in his eleven-year-old handwriting, *the candle taste*. The Law Hall had called it Grave-Lotus ash.

"Stop," Shen Mo said.

But the third breath was over, and the pill did what Earth-grade pills do at the end of the third breath. It dissolved on its own, all at once, and slid down his throat like warm oil.

"Stop *what*?" Kang said.

The first turn hit his marrow.

---

It was not pain at first. It was heat, deep and enormous, as though someone had lit a furnace inside his bones. He heard himself call it out, "*First turn. Heat in the long bones*," because nine years of protocol ran deeper than fear. The pulse-plate under his palm flared bright green, brighter than he had ever seen it.

Second turn. The heat went through his marrow like a flood through a dry riverbed and came out the other side into his meridians. There it met nine years of silt.

He felt it tear loose.

All of it at once: four thousand pills' worth of sediment, scoured out of the walls of his channels and churned into a grey tide. It was agony, and under the agony it was astonishing. For one breath, for the first time since he was ten, his qi *moved*, freely and fast, like a river that had forgotten it was a river.

"Third turn," he gasped. "Fourth—"

"His dregs are shearing," Lin Qiao said sharply. "Elder, the marrow-wash is too strong for a silted body. It's pulling everything loose together."

"Yes," said Gu, with interest. "Watch the plate."

Fifth turn.

The candle taste woke up.

It had been waiting, Shen Mo understood distantly. It had sat quiet in the marrow-broth while the pill opened his bones, and now that everything was open it went for his heart like a knife finding a gap in armor. His heart stumbled once, then again. The green light under his palm went grey, dirty grey, the colour of the Dregs Pit.

"Grave—" he said. His tongue was too thick. "Grave-l—"

The plate went black.

He was on the floor. He could hear, though. Hearing was the last thing to go; he had learned that on other mornings, beside other tasters. He heard Xu Ping make a small sound and heard someone hit the boy to quiet him. He heard the Elder's robes whisper as he knelt, and felt two cool fingers settle on his wrist.

"Heart meridian collapse at the fifth turn," said Elder Gu. There was no cruelty in it. There was nothing in it at all. "The marrow-wash tore the sediment loose all at once and the heart couldn't clear it. As I said: interesting. Record it, Disciple Lin. *Silted subject, failure at fifth turn, inconclusive.*"

"Elder." Lin Qiao's voice was strange and tight. "He said something. *Grave*—"

"They always say something at the end." The fingers withdrew. "Steward. Tomorrow, first bell, test the remainder of the batch on the clean boy at a third of the dose. A reduced dose will tell us what we need to know."

"And this one, Elder?"

A pause, no longer than it took to set down a weight.

"The pit."

---

In the dark there was no hall and no body, only the map.

Shen Mo saw his meridians the way he had always imagined them when he wrote in the ledger at night: a vast, branching network, like the roots of a tree or the rivers of a province. It was all grey now, every channel swirling with loosened dregs, drifting, with nowhere to go.

He knew them.

This fine grey drift in the lower left channels was this morning's Spleen-Warming Pill, too much licorice. That coarse brown sediment was Bone-Knitting Paste, the winter he was thirteen, when he'd tasted it for a month straight and his wrists ached for a year. That black grit was the Blood-Clotting batch, age eleven, three dead on the benches beside him.

Entry after entry. Page after page. Four hundred and twelve pages.

And as he named each one, it stopped drifting.

It turned, the way a fish turns in a current, and it began to sink, not outward into his flesh but inward and down, toward the place below his navel where his dantian sat cold and nearly empty. Grain after grain. Pill after pill. It gathered there and began, slowly, heavily, to *turn*.

Then even the map went out.

---

He woke to the smell of ash and rot and wet stone, and to the cold, which was total.

He was lying on a heap of furnace slag. Far above, a ragged oval of stars showed between walls of glassy black cinder.

His heart was beating. Slowly and heavily, like a man carrying something uphill, but beating.

"Breathing on his own," said a voice. "Now that's new."

He turned his head. An old woman was crouched on the slag an arm's length away. She was tiny, wrapped in scorched sacking, with milky, sightless eyes and an iron poker she had apparently been using to prod him.

Granny Fu. Every taster knew her, or knew of her: the blind madwoman who lived in a hut of broken furnace bricks at the edge of the Dregs Pit and sifted the sect's refuse for things to sell.

"I was dead," he said. His voice came out as a croak.

"You were thrown away. It's not the same thing, though the Pill Hall rarely checks." She sniffed the air near his face like a dog. "You stink of dregs, boy. More than anything I've smelled come down that chute in forty years. And yet." She leaned closer. "They're not in your meridians any more. Are they?"

Shen Mo breathed in.

Qi came, thin and cold from the night air, and it *went in*. It flowed through channels that had been choked for seven years, down and around and into his dantian. It poured without catching or dragging, like water down a clean gutter. He felt the threshold of the second layer of Qi Condensation, the wall he had stood in front of since he was ten, go past him in the dark like a milestone on a road.

And in the center of his dantian, where the qi gathered, something turned: grey, heavy, no bigger than a lotus seed, rotating slowly with the patience of a millstone.

"There's a—"

"Don't tell me what it looks like." Granny Fu's voice was suddenly sharp. "I don't want to know. Knowing things in this sect gets you thrown down here." She sat back on her heels. "Your book's in your robe. It's wet. I didn't read it."

"You're blind."

"That," she said, "is not the reason."

His hand went to his ribs. The ledger was there, damp and swollen, still stitched. He pulled it out and opened it to the last page by starlight, and the morning came back to him with it: the ivory pill, nine rings, bone broth and iron, and underneath it all the snuffed-wick bitterness of Grave-Lotus ash.

It had not been in the formula. He had named every ingredient, and Lin Qiao had written each one down without a flicker of surprise, right up to the toasted lotus seed, which had surprised her very much. If Grave-Lotus had been hers, she would have been waiting for him to name it.

It wasn't hers. Someone had put it there.

Elder Gu thought the pill had failed because Shen Mo was silted. Elder Gu had ordered the rest of the batch, same furnace and same poison, tested on Xu Ping at first bell. After that, in ten days, the pill would go to Senior Brother Pei Wenyuan, the golden son of the Azure Furnace Sect, at the moment his marrow was most open.

And Grave-Lotus ash was kept in only one place on the whole mountain: the Pill Hall's sealed stores.

Shen Mo found the brush-case in his sleeve. The ink had run, but there was enough. He wrote, small and neat, the way he had written three thousand, nine hundred and sixty-one times before:

*Entry 3,962. Nine-Turn Marrow Pill. Pill Hall trial batch, Earth grade. Taste: blood-marrow ginseng (40 yr), tiger-bone vine (fresh), white jade lotus seed (toasted), north-well water, nine-leaf dragon's-tongue. And Grave-Lotus ash. Effect: death.*

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he added a note.

*Not the formula. Someone put it there.*

He closed the book and looked up at the walls of the pit: sixty feet of glassy black slag, slick with runoff, glittering faintly in the starlight.

"First bell's in four hours," said Granny Fu, as though she could see him looking. "Dead men don't climb out of the Dregs Pit, boy."

Shen Mo tucked the ledger back against his ribs and set his palm on the cold slag. Deep in his dantian the grey seed turned once, slow and heavy, and he felt something he had never felt in nine years of swallowing other people's medicine.

He felt hungry.

"No," he agreed, and began to climb. "But I'm not dead. I've only been thrown away."
