# Chapter 1: The Sky Has No Top

Nobody in Thunderfall knew how high the sky went. The pickers said it went as far as Heaven, and Heaven was the green land whose edge hung over the western horizon on clear mornings, trailing waterfalls that turned to mist long before they reached the ground. The songs said there were nine heavens. The storyteller on Kiln Street said thirty-three, and charged a copper to hear their names. The mad monk at the broken shrine said the sky had no top at all. Ling Xiao, who was sixteen and had never been higher than the roof of the kiln-master's house, meant to find out which of them was lying.

He suspected all of them.

This morning Heaven was showing off. Its rim ran across the western sky like the edge of a lid somebody had left ajar, dark with forest, and the Veils poured off it in white ropes, a dozen of them, each one wider than Thunderfall and falling for so long that you forgot they were falling. The shadow of the whole thing lay across the western Dust in a permanent afternoon. Above it, the sky kept going. Blue, then a deeper blue, then a blue so high and thin it hurt to look at, and nothing in it at all. People said Immortals lived up on the green land and the lamps of the Jade Hall lived above that, and that was the end of the matter.

Ling Xiao didn't think it was the end of anything. He thought that if you could see a thing's edge, it had a far side, and if it had a far side, someone ought to go and look.

At the moment, he was sitting on a red-painted stake at the edge of the Anvil, eating a cold scallion pancake and planning to rob a tribulation.

"You're not going to rob a tribulation," Guo Bao said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You're doing the face." Guo Bao was fifteen and built like a rice barrel, which was why the whole of Thunderfall called him Dumpling and why he had never once objected. He was holding a lidded clay jar against his chest the way other boys held puppies. "The face you did before the thing with the Governor's ducks."

"The ducks were a triumph."

"The ducks bit me."

Below them the Anvil stretched out to the horizon: a plain of black glass forty li across, melted and re-fused a thousand times, glittering in the morning like a frozen sea. Nothing grew on it. Nothing ever would. Cultivators came down from Heaven to be struck by lightning here, because a tribulation could flatten a mountain and nobody wanted that happening on their own expensive land. So they descended to the Dust, did their dying or their rising on the Anvil, and went home. The mortals of Thunderfall lived off the leftovers.

Four hundred red stakes marked the safe line. Behind it, the scorch-pickers were gathering in their hundreds: old women with sieves, children with sacks, men with hooked poles for dragging treasure out of craters that were still too hot to walk in. There would be lightning-glass after today, ore fused into strange new alloys, and if the candidate failed there would be the shattered pieces of whatever magic sword or armor had failed with her. Scorch-picking paid better than farming. It also killed more people, which kept the competition down.

A notice had been nailed to the stake beside Ling Xiao's that morning, the paper still crackling with a faint blue charge:

*By order of the Thunder Court. At the hour of the horse, Mei Canglan of the Cloudsea Sword Sect will undergo the Tribulation of Core Formation upon the Anvil. Mortals will remain beyond the red stakes. Heaven does not miss. Neither does the Court.*

"Cloudsea," Dumpling said. He'd been frowning at it since dawn. "Where's Cloudsea?"

"Up."

"Everything's up. Normally it says which sect on Heaven. *Verdant Pine, of Heaven. Ironbark, of Heaven.* This just says Cloudsea."

"Then it's somewhere on Heaven they didn't bother to tell us about." Ling Xiao said it easily, but he'd read the notice six times, and every time the missing words had itched at him like a splinter.

A Core Formation tribulation. Nine bolts. He had watched forty-one of them in his life, and he could still feel his heart speeding up, as if it knew something the rest of him didn't. It always had. When he was small, Auntie Guo used to find him on the shop roof in thunderstorms with his face turned up and his mouth open, like a chick waiting to be fed. She'd drag him down by the ear and tell him, every time, that the sky had already had one go at him and he shouldn't give it a second.

He'd been born at the edge of the Anvil, down at the Scorched Corner where the old picker shacks used to stand. Sixteen years ago, on a cloudless afternoon, a bolt had come out of an empty sky. No tribulation, no candidate, no spiral of cloud; just one white line from nowhere. It hit a shack where two strangers had taken shelter, a man and a woman nobody in Thunderfall had ever seen, and when the pickers dug through the glass, the strangers were dead and their newborn son was lying in the middle of the crater, screaming, without a burn on him. All that was left of them was a scorched swaddling cloth with two characters embroidered in silver thread: 凌霄. *Above the clouds.*

The storyteller on Kiln Street told it as an omen. The pickers told it as a joke. Ling Xiao told it as little as possible. He didn't hate the sky for it. You couldn't hate weather. But he had spent his whole life wondering what kind of sky threw lightning with no clouds in it, and where exactly it had been thrown *from*.

"Walk me through it again," Dumpling said, "so I can explain it to my mother at the funeral."

Ling Xiao licked scallion oil off his thumb. "The Skygate opens in thirty-one days."

"I know that."

"To climb it, you need a token. A token costs a hundred spirit stones. We have four spirit stones, one of which is chipped."

"I know *that*."

"And I've been stuck at the third layer of Qi Condensation for two years." He said it lightly. He always said it lightly. Every child in Thunderfall learned the Stone-Breath Method from the characters carved on the west wall, and ninety-nine in a hundred reached the third layer and stopped there forever, breathing air too thin to feed them. But Ling Xiao's trouble wasn't only thin air. He could feel qi when he breathed; it just wouldn't *stay*. It slid off him like rain off oilcloth. He'd tried everything. He had once tried eating powdered lightning-glass, and he did not recommend it. "Up there the air is thick enough to drown in qi. If anything can make it stick to me, that can. And if it can't, I'd still rather be stuck up there than down here. So. A hundred stones."

"And the ninth bolt."

"And the ninth bolt." Ling Xiao pointed, not at the sky, but at the long bundle wrapped in sacking at his feet. "The last bolt of a tribulation always leaves a thunder-heart where it grounds. You know what a thunder-heart the size of a walnut is worth?"

"More than both of us."

"Three hundred stones in the Ghost Market. The candidate's sect always takes it, because the last bolt always grounds under the candidate. *Except*," he said, and here he tapped the bundle, "sword cultivators. A sword cultivator meets the ninth bolt blade first. It forks off the steel. I've seen it happen eleven times. The fork goes to the nearest metal, and that is going to be my spike."

"Which will be inside the stakes."

"About two hundred paces inside the stakes."

"During a tribulation."

"Between the eighth and ninth bolts. There's always a pause. Heaven takes a breath."

Dumpling looked at him for a long moment. "Heaven," he said, "does not need to breathe."

"Then it's being polite. Either way, I'll have time."

---

The Iron Crows arrived an hour before noon, the way they always did: eight of them in black oilcloth, walking the stake line and tapping pickers on the shoulder. The first three rows along the eastern edge, where the best craters formed, belonged to the Crows. Everyone knew that. Everyone paid.

The man who stopped in front of Ling Xiao's stake was called Big Tan, and he was big, and he was at the fifth layer of Qi Condensation, which in Thunderfall made him a small god.

"Off," Big Tan said.

"Morning, Brother Tan. Lovely day for it."

"This is Crow ground. Off, or pay."

"I'd love to pay. I've got a chipped spirit stone with your name on it. Well, not literally. I could scratch it on."

A few pickers laughed. Big Tan did not like that. His hand closed on Ling Xiao's collar and lifted him off the stake as easily as lifting a cat, and the first punch came in low and fast, a fist wrapped in fifth-layer qi. Ling Xiao got an arm down in time, which only meant the blow folded the arm into his ribs instead of breaking them. White light went through him. He hit the glass rolling, tasted blood, and came up on one knee.

He'd known it would hurt. He'd been counting on Tan being angry.

"Row three's yours anyway," he wheezed. "Everybody says so. They say it's the only row you can count to."

Big Tan roared and came at him. Ling Xiao didn't run backward. He ran sideways, three quick steps across the glass, onto the pale grey patch he'd been sitting beside all morning. Under his feet the Anvil rang a half-tone flat, the way it did only in one place. Old bolts sometimes left bubbles under the glass, big enough to stand in, capped by a crust as thin as a teacup. Pickers called it hollow glass. You learned the sound of it or you learned what it felt like to fall in.

Ling Xiao was light, and he kept moving. Big Tan was two hundred jin of angry man with fifth-layer qi pouring into his legs, and he planted his feet to throw his best punch.

The crust gave way with a sound like a temple bell breaking. Big Tan dropped to his armpits, wedged in the hole, legs kicking in the dark underneath. His fist, already swinging, hit nothing at all.

Ling Xiao crouched in front of him, careful to keep his feet on solid glass. His ribs were on fire. He smiled anyway.

"I'm moving," he said. "You can keep the stake."

The laughter this time was loud and long. Up and down the line, pickers who had been paying the Crows for years were grinning. Big Tan's face went purple. Two of the other Crows started forward.

Then someone at the far end of the line shouted, and every face on the Anvil turned upward. Not west, toward Heaven.

Straight up.

---

She came out of the empty blue.

Every candidate Ling Xiao had ever watched had come from the west: a streak off Heaven's rim, a flying sword sliding down past the Veils, a cloud-barge easing out of the green land's shadow. This was a thread of light falling from directly overhead, out of the high thin nothing where there was supposed to be nothing, and it fell for longer than anything should be able to fall. Ten breaths. Twenty. Long enough for the whole picker line to go silent. Long enough for Ling Xiao to understand, with a lurch in his stomach like missing a step in the dark, that wherever it had started, it was higher than Heaven.

It stopped dead a finger's width above the center of the Anvil. When the glare faded, a woman stood there. At this distance she was a sliver of grey-blue robe and black hair. Her sword wasn't on her back. It hung in the air beside her, point down, turning slowly as though it were looking around.

"That's not from Heaven," Dumpling whispered. He had come to crouch beside Ling Xiao with the jar. "Xiao. That didn't come from Heaven."

"No," Ling Xiao said. His mouth had gone dry, and he was grinning so hard it hurt his split lip. "It came from *above* it."

Mei Canglan of the Cloudsea Sword Sect sat cross-legged on the glass, laid the sword across her knees, and closed her eyes.

The clouds began to turn.

He'd seen it forty-one times and it still stopped his breath. The sky above the Anvil pulled itself into a spiral, slowly at first and then faster, until a whirlpool of black cloud the width of a province was grinding above them. The light went brown, then green, then a color that didn't have a name. Along the rim of the spiral, very high up, a single white figure stood on a cloud with a brush and a scroll: a Warden of the Thunder Court, there to count.

"Xiao, I changed my mind about the ninth bolt."

"Too late. I've already spent the money."

The first bolt fell.

It was as thick as an old tree and bright enough that Ling Xiao saw his own finger bones through his eyelids. Mei Canglan rose to meet it with her sword, and the bolt broke across the blade and scattered in a hundred white snakes that tore across the glass. The sound arrived a heartbeat later. It went into Ling Xiao's chest and shook everything loose, and under the fear, somewhere down in his belly, something that had been asleep his whole life turned over and *leaned toward it*.

He noticed that. He filed it away for later, the way he filed away the sound of hollow glass.

The second bolt was thicker. The third was blue. The fourth came down in three strands braided together, and she cut two of them and took the third on her open palm, and the whole Anvil jumped. By the sixth her robe was smoking. By the seventh she was on one knee, and pickers all along the line were muttering the old odds: nine bolts for Core Formation, survive all nine and you become a Golden Core Immortal and live five hundred years, fail and the pickers eat well for a month.

The eighth bolt was purple, and it didn't strike so much as *lean* on her, a pillar of light that pressed down for a long, long count of ten. When it lifted, Mei Canglan was still there. Barely. Her sword was cracked. She raised it again anyway.

Then the sky went quiet.

Heaven took its breath.

Ling Xiao was already running.

He had the bundle under one arm and Dumpling's jar under the other, since Dumpling hadn't let go until the last possible moment, and he went through the red stakes and onto the Anvil and heard the whole picker line gasp behind him. The glass was hot enough to feel through his sandals. Fifty paces. A hundred. Stray lightning crawled along the ground in slow blue worms that he jumped over. At a hundred and ninety paces he could feel the air thickening, the pressure of the spiral like a hand on his head. At two hundred he dropped to his knees, ripped the sacking off the spike, and drove it into a crack in the glass.

It was sky-iron, an arm's length long, black as a burned fingernail, and it had never once been cold in the three years he'd owned it. A flood had carried it out of the east, from the Ruin people said, and dropped it in a crater where he'd dug it out. Sometimes at night it hummed. He'd never sold it because he'd always known what it was for. He jammed the clay jar over its foot, packed it round with the wet ash-mud he'd carried in his sleeves, and looked up.

Mei Canglan was staring at him.

She was fifty paces away, kneeling, blood on her chin, and her eyes were perfectly clear. For a moment the two of them looked at each other across the black glass, a cultivator from somewhere higher than Heaven and a scorch-picker from the Dust, and Ling Xiao had the strangest feeling that she knew exactly what he was doing and was deciding whether she cared.

Then the ninth bolt came down.

It didn't fall. It *arrived*. There was no light before it and no sound; there was simply a river of white connecting sky to earth, and Mei Canglan was standing in it with her sword held high, and the sword split it the way Ling Xiao had watched eleven swords split it, and a fork of lightning as thick as his waist leapt off the blade toward the nearest metal.

The spike was the nearest metal.

Ling Xiao was standing next to the spike.

He had calculated the fork. He had not calculated how fast it would be.

---

It should have been like being burned. He'd been burned plenty of times. He waited, in the white place where his body had been, to be ash.

The lightning went looking for something to burn and found only more lightning.

That was the only way he could ever explain it afterward. The bolt poured into him expecting meat and bone and a boy's sixteen years, and it found something it recognized, something made of the same bright stuff, and it hesitated. And in that hesitation, from the bottom of Ling Xiao's belly, from the place that had leaned toward the first bolt, rose a hunger he'd carried his whole life without ever having a name for it. It was the feeling of every thunderstorm on the shop roof. It was the feeling of breathing qi that wouldn't stay. It opened like a mouth.

*Oh,* he thought. *Oh, so* this *is what I eat.*

He ate.

---

He came back to himself on his hands and knees, and the lightning was *inside* him.

It was in his meridians, every one of them at once. For two years his qi had crawled through those channels like cold soup and slid out of him again. Now something went through them like a flood through a dry riverbed, and it didn't slide out. It *stuck*. It sank into him the way water sinks into sand that has waited all summer for rain, and the walls he'd been beating his head against since he was fourteen simply weren't there anymore.

The fourth layer of Qi Condensation broke open like a door kicked off its hinges.

He screamed, or laughed. He couldn't tell. The fifth layer came before he'd finished feeling the fourth: the lightning found the next wall and went through it, and his bones rang like the Anvil, and his lungs took in air and the air was *full*, full of qi he'd never been able to hold before, as if he'd been breathing through a cloth his whole life and someone had pulled it off his face. The sixth layer cracked and gave. His skin was smoking. His hair was standing straight up. There was blood coming out of his nose and he didn't care, because he could hear the qi in the ground now, humming.

At the seventh layer, the flood hit a wall that held.

Not the lightning's limit. His. It was too much, too heavy, meant for someone about to live five hundred years, and he could feel his body refusing the rest the way a stomach refuses the tenth bowl. What he couldn't swallow drew back from the edges of him and sank into his dantian below his navel. It lay there like a sword in a sheath, cold and heavy and humming, and it was *sharp*. When he breathed too deep, it cut. It tasted cold and exact and proud, and after a moment he recognized the taste. It was the look on Mei Canglan's face.

And under that taste, so faint he almost missed it, there was another one.

Older. Not cold, not proud, not anything he had words for. It had been in him, he realized, the whole time; he'd only never had anything to compare it to. It tasted like a clear sky. It tasted like the far side of something.

He knelt there on the black glass and held very still, trying to hold on to it, and it faded the way a word fades when you've said it too many times.

Sixth layer. In three breaths he had climbed further than the Stone-Breath Method had taken anyone in Thunderfall in a generation. He opened his hands and looked at them. They were steady. There was faint blue light crawling in the creases of his knuckles, and when he closed his fists, it went out.

"Oh," Ling Xiao said out loud, to nobody. "Oh, that's *interesting*."

---

The jar at the foot of the spike had cracked in half. The spike itself had melted into a black puddle, the first time in three years it had ever gone cold. But in the cooling ash-mud, exactly where he'd packed it, sat a pearl the size of a walnut, pale violet and crawling with tiny threads of light.

A thunder-heart. Not a big one. It looked, if he was honest, a little *nibbled*. But it was enough.

He scooped it up and tucked it inside his shirt, where it buzzed against his ribs like a trapped bee. Then he stood up. His legs held him. His legs held him *easily*. Behind him, four hundred red stakes' worth of scorch-pickers were making a noise he'd never heard a crowd make before, a low, stunned roar, and somewhere in it he could hear Dumpling yelling his name.

In front of him, in the center of a crater that was still glowing, Mei Canglan was sitting up.

She had done it. He could feel it even from here: something new and dense and golden turning inside her, a Core, pulling qi toward itself like a whirlpool. She had survived her tribulation. But she wasn't celebrating. She was looking at her own hands, then at the sky, then at him, and her face was very white.

Ling Xiao understood the look a moment later, because the cold thing in his belly understood it first. It turned in its sheath toward her, the way a needle turns north. It knew whose it was.

Nine bolts made a flawless core. The ninth had broken on her sword, and the part of it that mattered was in him.

The spiral in the sky was unwinding. Out of it, down a stair of cloud that formed under her feet as she walked, came the white figure from its rim: a woman in the plain white robe of the Thunder Court, with a brush tucked behind one ear and a scroll in her hand. Her feet touched the Anvil without a sound. The pressure that came off her made the sixth layer of Qi Condensation feel like nothing at all. Core Formation at least. Probably more. Maybe a great deal more; from down here he had no way to measure the top of anyone.

She unrolled the scroll and read from it in a voice that carried across forty li of glass without her raising it.

"Nine bolts fell," said the Warden. "The candidate received eight."

She lowered the scroll. Her gaze went across the Anvil: across the crater and the cultivator in it, across the picker line and the red stakes, across the black puddle where a spike of sky-iron had been.

It stopped on him.

Ling Xiao put his hand flat over the cold place under his navel and smiled the smile that had gotten him out of trouble exactly as often as it had gotten him into it.

Above all of them, above the Warden and the swordswoman and the green rim of Heaven and whatever lay beyond it, the sky went on up, as far as it liked. For the first time in his life, Ling Xiao knew for certain that something up there had an answer for him.

He was going to go and get it.
