# Chapter 1: Put It On My Tab

There were nine heavens above Thunderfall, and on a clear morning Ling Xiao could see every one of them.

The First Heaven hung lowest, a green continent riding the sky a hundred li up, so vast that its shadow kept the western half of the Dust in permanent afternoon. Rivers poured off its edges in white ropes and thinned to mist long before they reached the ground. Above it and further east floated the Second Heaven in its own private sea of cloud; above that the Third, red as a banked coal; then the ragged gap where the Fourth used to be; and then the rest of them, climbing away into the blue, smaller and brighter, until the Ninth was only a point of light you might mistake for a star if you didn't know better.

Ling Xiao knew better. He'd been named after the palace on it.

His mother had done that on purpose. Lingxiao Hall was where the Sovereign of the Ninth Heaven was said to sit, at the top of everything, and Ma Tang had looked at her squalling son in a rented room that smelled of ash and decided he should have it. "If you're going to be born in the Dust," she liked to say, between coughs, "you should at least have a good address."

Sixteen years later, the good address was sitting on a red-painted stake at the edge of the Anvil, eating a cold scallion pancake and planning to rob Heaven.

"You're not going to rob Heaven," Guo Bao said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You're doing the face." Guo Bao was fifteen and built like a rice barrel, which was why the whole of Thunderfall called him Dumpling and why he had never once objected. He was holding a lidded clay jar against his chest the way other boys held puppies. "The face you did before the thing with the Governor's ducks."

"The ducks were a triumph."

"The ducks bit me."

Below them the Anvil stretched out to the horizon: a plain of black glass forty li across, melted and re-fused a thousand times, glittering in the morning like a frozen sea. Nothing grew on it. Nothing ever would. Cultivators from every heaven came down here to be struck by lightning, because a tribulation could flatten a mountain and nobody wanted that happening on their own expensive continent. So they descended to the Dust, did their dying or their rising on the Anvil, and went home. The mortals of Thunderfall lived off the leftovers.

Four hundred red stakes marked the safe line. Behind it, the scorch-pickers were gathering in their hundreds: old women with sieves, children with sacks, men with hooked poles for dragging treasure out of craters that were still too hot to walk in. There would be lightning-glass after today, ore fused into strange new alloys, and if the candidate failed there would be the shattered pieces of whatever magic sword or armor had failed with her. Scorch-picking paid better than farming. It also killed more people, which kept the competition down.

A notice had been nailed to the stake beside Ling Xiao's that morning, the paper still crackling with a faint blue charge:

*By order of the Thunder Court. At the hour of the horse, Mei Canglan of the Cloudsea Sword Sect, Second Heaven, will undergo the Tribulation of Core Formation upon the Anvil. Mortals will remain beyond the red stakes. Heaven does not miss. Neither does the Court.*

A Core Formation tribulation. Nine bolts. Ling Xiao had watched forty-one of them in his life and he could still feel his heart speeding up, as if it knew something the rest of him didn't.

"Walk me through it again," Dumpling said, "so I can explain it to your mother at the funeral."

Ling Xiao licked scallion oil off his thumb. "The Skygate opens in thirty-one days."

"I know that."

"To climb it, you need a token. A token costs a hundred spirit stones. I have four spirit stones, one of which is chipped."

"I know *that*."

"And the air on the First Heaven is thick enough with qi to heal ash-lung in a season. Only family of heavenly disciples get residency." He said it lightly. He'd been saying it lightly for two years, ever since Ma's cough had gone from a winter thing to an every-season thing, and then to the kind of cough that left spots on her sleeve she thought he didn't see. "So. A hundred stones."

"And the ninth bolt."

"And the ninth bolt." Ling Xiao pointed, not at the sky, but at the long bundle wrapped in sacking at his feet. "The last bolt of a tribulation always leaves a thunder-heart where it grounds. You know what a thunder-heart the size of a walnut is worth?"

"More than both of us."

"Three hundred stones in the Ghost Market. The candidate's sect always takes it, because the last bolt always grounds under the candidate. *Except*," he said, and here he tapped the bundle, "sword cultivators. A sword cultivator meets the ninth bolt blade first. It forks off the steel. I've seen it happen eleven times. The fork goes to the nearest metal, and that is going to be my spike."

"Which will be inside the stakes."

"About two hundred paces inside the stakes."

"During a tribulation."

"Between the eighth and ninth bolts. There's always a pause. Heaven takes a breath."

Dumpling looked at him for a long moment. "Heaven," he said, "does not need to breathe."

"Then it's being polite. Either way, I'll have time."

---

The Iron Crows arrived an hour before noon, the way they always did: eight of them in black oilcloth, walking the stake line and tapping pickers on the shoulder. The first three rows along the eastern edge, where the best craters formed, belonged to the Crows. Everyone knew that. Everyone paid.

The man who stopped in front of Ling Xiao's stake was called Big Tan, and he was big, and he was at the fifth layer of Qi Condensation, which in Thunderfall made him a small god. The Dust didn't have enough qi in it to take anyone much further. Every child in the city learned the Stone-Breath Method from the characters carved into the west wall, and ninety-nine in a hundred of them reached the third layer and stopped there, forever, breathing air too thin to feed them.

Ling Xiao had been stuck at the third layer for two years. He had tried everything. He had once tried eating powdered lightning-glass, and he did not recommend it.

"Off," Big Tan said.

"Morning, Brother Tan. Lovely day for it."

"This is Crow ground. Off, or pay."

"I'd love to pay. I've got a chipped spirit stone with your name on it. Well, not literally. I could scratch it on."

A few pickers laughed. Big Tan did not like that. His hand closed on Ling Xiao's collar and lifted him off the stake as easily as lifting a cat, and the first punch came in low and fast, a fist wrapped in fifth-layer qi. Ling Xiao got an arm down in time, which only meant the blow folded the arm into his ribs instead of breaking them. White light went through him. He hit the glass rolling, tasted blood, and came up on one knee.

He'd known it would hurt. He'd been counting on Tan being angry.

"Row three's yours anyway," he wheezed. "Everybody says so. They say it's the only row you can count to."

Big Tan roared and came at him. Ling Xiao didn't run backward. He ran sideways, three quick steps across the glass, onto the pale grey patch he'd been sitting beside all morning. Under his feet the Anvil rang a half-tone flat, the way it did only in one place. Old bolts sometimes left bubbles under the glass, big enough to stand in, capped by a crust as thin as a teacup. Pickers called it hollow glass. You learned the sound of it or you learned what it felt like to fall in.

Ling Xiao was light, and he kept moving. Big Tan was two hundred jin of angry man with fifth-layer qi pouring into his legs, and he planted his feet to throw his best punch.

The crust gave way with a sound like a temple bell breaking. Big Tan dropped to his armpits, wedged in the hole, legs kicking in the dark underneath. His fist, already swinging, hit nothing at all.

Ling Xiao crouched in front of him, careful to keep his feet on solid glass. His ribs were on fire. He smiled anyway.

"I'm moving," he said. "You can keep the stake."

The laughter this time was loud and long. Up and down the line, pickers who had been paying the Crows for years were grinning. Big Tan's face went purple. Two of the other Crows started forward. Then the sky changed, and every face on the Anvil turned upward.

---

She came down from the Second Heaven like a star that had decided to stop.

A thread of blue light fell out of the cloud-sea overhead, fell for longer than seemed possible, and stopped dead a finger's width above the center of the Anvil. When the glare faded, a woman stood there. At this distance she was a sliver of grey-blue robe and black hair. Her sword wasn't on her back. It hung in the air beside her, point down, turning slowly as though it were looking around.

Mei Canglan of the Cloudsea Sword Sect sat cross-legged on the glass, laid the sword across her knees, and closed her eyes.

The clouds began to turn.

Ling Xiao had seen it forty-one times and it still stopped his breath. The sky above the Anvil pulled itself into a spiral, slowly at first and then faster, until a whirlpool of black cloud the width of a province was grinding above them. The light went brown, then green, then a color that didn't have a name. Along the rim of the spiral, very high up, a single white figure stood on a cloud with a brush and a scroll: a Warden of the Thunder Court, there to count.

"Hour of the horse," Dumpling whispered. He had come to crouch beside Ling Xiao with the jar. "Xiao. Xiao, I changed my mind about the ninth bolt."

"Too late. I've already spent the money."

The first bolt fell.

It was as thick as an old tree and bright enough that Ling Xiao saw his own finger bones through his eyelids. Mei Canglan rose to meet it with her sword, and the bolt broke across the blade and scattered in a hundred white snakes that tore across the glass. The sound arrived a heartbeat later. It went into Ling Xiao's chest and shook everything loose.

The second bolt was thicker. The third was blue. The fourth came down in three strands braided together, and she cut two of them and took the third on her open palm, and the whole Anvil jumped. By the sixth her robe was smoking. By the seventh she was on one knee, and pickers all along the line were muttering the old odds: nine bolts for Core Formation, survive all nine and your foundation becomes a golden core and your life runs five hundred years, fail and the pickers eat well for a month.

The eighth bolt was purple, and it didn't strike so much as *lean* on her, a pillar of light that pressed down for a long, long count of ten. When it lifted, Mei Canglan was still there. Barely. Her sword was cracked. She raised it again anyway.

Then the sky went quiet.

Heaven took its breath.

Ling Xiao was already running.

He had the bundle under one arm and Dumpling's jar under the other, since Dumpling hadn't let go until the last possible moment, and he went through the red stakes and onto the Anvil and heard the whole picker line gasp behind him. The glass was hot enough to feel through his sandals. Fifty paces. A hundred. Stray lightning crawled along the ground in slow blue worms that he jumped over. At a hundred and ninety paces he could feel the air thickening, the pressure of the spiral like a hand on his head. He counted it by the sound his heart was making. At two hundred he dropped to his knees, ripped the sacking off the spike, and drove it into a crack in the glass.

It was heaven-iron, an arm's length long and black as a fingernail after a burn. He'd dug it out of a crater three years ago, the only real treasure he'd ever found, and had never sold it because he'd always known what it was for. He jammed the clay jar over its foot, packed it round with the wet ash-mud he'd carried in his sleeves, and looked up.

Mei Canglan was staring at him.

She was fifty paces away, kneeling, blood on her chin, and her eyes were perfectly clear. For a moment the two of them looked at each other across the black glass, a Foundation cultivator of the Second Heaven and a scorch-picker from the Dust, and Ling Xiao had the strangest feeling that she knew exactly what he was doing, and was deciding whether she cared.

Then the ninth bolt came down.

It didn't fall. It *arrived*. There was no light before it and no sound; there was simply a river of white connecting heaven to earth, and Mei Canglan was standing in it with her sword held high, and the sword split it the way Ling Xiao had watched eleven swords split it, and a fork of lightning as thick as his waist leapt off the blade toward the nearest metal.

The spike was the nearest metal.

Ling Xiao was standing next to the spike.

He had calculated the fork. He had not calculated how fast it would be.

---

It was not like being burned. He'd been burned plenty of times. This was like being *read*.

Something enormous had opened him up and was turning the pages. It went through him in an instant and it took forever. It weighed every breath of qi he had ever drawn from the world, every mouthful of rice, every cup of his mother's bitter tea, sixteen years of taking. It set all that on one side of a scale, and on the other side it set the lightning, and the lightning was heavier. Of course it was heavier. It was meant for someone about to live five hundred years. He was a boy with a chipped spirit stone.

The answer was obvious. Ash. A smudge on the glass for the pickers to step around.

Ling Xiao understood, in the white place where his body had been, that he was being asked a question. Not in words. Heaven didn't need words. The question was simple: *You have taken this. How will you pay?*

He thought of his mother coughing in the dark. He thought of the Ninth Heaven, a star you could mistake for a star. He thought, with a clarity he would never quite be able to explain afterward, that he was not going to die on his knees in the Dust, owing money.

*I don't have it on me,* he told Heaven. *Put it on my tab.*

A pause. The longest pause of his life.

*I'm good for it,* he added. *I'm coming up anyway. I'll pay you at the top.*

And somewhere unimaginably high above him, above the Ninth Heaven, above anything with a name, something that had never once been argued with dipped a brush in ink and wrote him down.

---

He came back to himself on his hands and knees, and the lightning was *inside* him.

It was in his meridians, every one of them at once. For two years his qi had crawled through those channels like cold soup. Now something went through them like a flood through a dry riverbed, and the walls he'd been beating his head against since he was fourteen simply weren't there anymore.

The fourth layer of Qi Condensation broke open like a door kicked off its hinges.

He screamed, or laughed. He couldn't tell. The fifth layer came before he'd finished feeling the fourth: the lightning found the next wall and went through it, and his bones rang like the Anvil, and his lungs took in air and the air was *full*, full of qi he'd never been able to feel before, as if he'd been breathing through a cloth his whole life and someone had pulled it off his face. The sixth layer cracked and gave. His skin was smoking. His hair was standing straight up. There was blood coming out of his nose and he didn't care, because he could hear the qi in the ground now, humming.

At the seventh layer, the flood hit a wall that held.

Not the lightning's limit. His. His body couldn't take any more; he could feel it the way you feel a bowl about to overflow. The rest of the bolt, and there was so much rest, drew back from the edges of him and coiled down into his dantian below his navel. It lay there like a sword in a sheath, humming, and it was *sharp*. It carried a flavor he'd never tasted before, cold and exact and proud, and after a moment he recognized it. It tasted like the look on Mei Canglan's face.

Sixth layer. In the space of three breaths, he had climbed further than the Stone-Breath Method had taken anyone in Thunderfall in a generation.

He looked down at his left palm, because it was hurting in a way that was different from all the other hurting.

A mark had burned itself into the skin, black and glossy as fresh ink. A single stroke, slanting from the heel of his hand toward his fingers. It looked like the first line of a character whose meaning hadn't been decided yet.

When he touched it, it was cold. And in that cold, very faintly, he felt something waiting. Patient. The way a moneylender waits.

The ninth bolt hadn't gone anywhere. It was *his* now, the way a debt is yours. He knew, as surely as he knew his own name, that the day he broke through into Foundation Establishment, Heaven would send it back down to collect.

"Oh," Ling Xiao said out loud, to nobody. "Oh, that's *interesting*."

---

The jar at the foot of the spike had cracked in half. The spike itself had melted into a black puddle. But in the cooling ash-mud, exactly where he'd packed it, sat a pearl the size of a walnut, pale violet and crawling with tiny threads of light.

A thunder-heart. Not a big one; the fork had given most of itself to him. But enough.

He scooped it up with his right hand, the one without the mark, and tucked it inside his shirt, where it buzzed against his ribs like a trapped bee. Then he stood up. His legs held him. His legs held him *easily*. Behind him, four hundred red stakes' worth of scorch-pickers were making a noise he'd never heard a crowd make before, a sort of low, stunned roar, and somewhere in it he could hear Dumpling yelling his name.

In front of him, in the center of a crater that was still glowing, Mei Canglan was sitting up.

She had done it. He could feel it even from here: something new and dense and golden turning inside her, a Core, pulling qi toward itself like a whirlpool. She had survived her tribulation. But she wasn't celebrating. She was looking at her own hands, then at the sky, then at him, and her face was very white.

Ling Xiao understood the look a moment later. Nine bolts made a flawless core. She'd only received eight.

The spiral in the sky was unwinding. Out of it, down a stair of cloud that formed under her feet as she walked, came the white figure from its rim: a woman in the plain white robe of the Thunder Court, with a brush tucked behind one ear and a scroll in her hand. Her feet touched the Anvil without a sound. The pressure that came off her made the sixth layer of Qi Condensation feel like nothing at all. Core Formation, at least. Probably more.

She unrolled the scroll and read from it in a voice that carried across forty li of glass without her raising it.

"Nine bolts fell," said the Warden. "The candidate received eight."

She lowered the scroll. Her gaze went across the Anvil: across the crater and the cultivator in it, across the picker line and the red stakes, across the black puddle where a heaven-iron spike had been.

It stopped on him.

Ling Xiao closed his left hand into a fist, over a mark that was still cold, and smiled the smile that had gotten him out of trouble exactly as often as it had gotten him into it.

Somewhere above the Ninth Heaven, the ink was still drying.
